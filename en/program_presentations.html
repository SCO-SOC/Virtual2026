A Species Distribution Model to Conserve LeConte’s Sparrow in the Canadian Prairies

Presenter(s): Amy Sutley1, Alana Westwood2
Affiliation(s): 1. Dalhousie University, 2. Dalhousie University
Session: Session 2: Conservation Biology
Time: 2026-10-06 17:10 UTC

Abstract

The population of LeConte’s sparrow (Ammospiza leconteii) is declining across the portion of its breeding range which falls in Western Canada. LeConte’s sparrow has been identified as a priority for conservation assessment and management by Canadian government agencies, but the species is poorly understood in terms of its ecology and distribution. We developed a high-resolution species distribution model (SDM) of LeConte’s sparrow occurrence in the Prairie Ecozone of Canada to investigate the habitat associations and spatial distribution of LeConte’s sparrow and identify candidate areas for land conservation. A maximum entropy (MaxEnt) method was used to predict the probability of occurrence for LeConte’s sparrow. In the presence observations dataset, 2,595 of 8,253 (31%) occurrences may have represented positional uncertainty as they were located on roads and in open bodies of water. As most SDMs rarely correct for fine-scale positional uncertainty, we tested three different correction distances (relocating observations to adjacent habitat). We compared three different models (no relocations; relocations within 30m; relocations within 100m) and found that they performed similarly. Covariates used to predict occurrence included proximity to wetlands, land use, elevation, mean summer precipitation, and a normalized difference vegetation index. Model performance and output was similar across corrections, and we selected the 100m correction to maximize sample size. Probability of occurrence for LeConte’s sparrow showed a strong association with low elevation prairie wetlands and riparian areas, including urban rivers. To develop recommendations for land conservation, we identified seven priority locations where existing protected areas could be expanded to include potential habitat for LeConte’s sparrow. We encourage managers to field-validate and apply our model to inform habitat protection for this species.

An investigation into how Golden-crowned Sparrow (Zonotrichia atricapilla) movement and survival in overwintering habitats differs along an urbanization gradient

Presenter(s): Dilan Praat1, Dr David Green2, Dr Elizabeth Gow3
Affiliation(s): 1. Simon Fraser University, 2. Simon Fraser University, 3. Environment and Climate Change Canada
Session: 3MT - Urban Ecology
Time: 2026-10-06 20:44 UTC

Abstract

Urban habitats offer many unique opportunities and challenges to various bird species. With the global land-cover of cities expected to double over the next 25 years, it is crucial to understand how birds are surviving and moving in urban landscapes. Past research on urban settings has focused on the survival and reproductive success of breeding birds. There is significantly less research into the movement and survival of songbirds overwintering in urban areas. This discrepancy is important as many species spend large portions of their life-cycle in there wintering ranges, and this may also be the time when food and predation pressures are at there highest. We studied how Golden-crowned sparrow Zonotrichia atricapilla movement and survival differs from rural parks to urban neighborhoods in Metro-Vancouver. 30 birds were radio-tagged across five sites in the winter of 2025-2026, and an additional 200+ birds were colour-banded across six sites from 2021-2026. We tracked the radio-tagged birds for six weeks to obtain details on fine-scale movements to determine habitat use and home range size, and to ascertain known-fate survival. Reported colour-band resightings were used to estimate broad overwintering Golden-crowned sparrow apparent-survival. We found that areas of increased urbanization had lower rates of known-fate survival and higher rates of unexplained disappearances. Birds initially caught in the proximity of static bird feeders had smaller home ranges than the home ranges of birds that were not caught in close proximity to feeders. Through colour-band resightings, we found that birds overwintering in areas with higher rates of urbanization had lower rates of apparent-survival than those living in rural areas. These findings indicate that while urban habitats are suitable for overwintering Golden-crowned sparrows, these benefits are mitigated by potential survival risks, and more research is needed to understand the mechanics of these relationships.

Assessing Population Trends and Structure of a Cryptic Waterbird Across its Range

Presenter(s): Joe Brosseau1, Joseph Brosseau2
Affiliation(s): 1. Biology MSc Student, University of Saskatchewan, 2. Department of Biology, University of Saskatchewan
Session: 3MT - Ecology: Populations
Time: 2026-10-06 20:24 UTC

Abstract

The Forster’s Tern (Sterna forsteri) is an understudied North American waterbird with an indeterminate population status in potential decline. Compared to other North American Tern species, Forster’s Terns have shorter migrations, staying within North America throughout the annual cycle. The core of the Forster’s Tern breeding range is in interior wetlands of the Prairie Pothole Region, with additional breeding populations ranging across the southern Atlantic and Gulf of Mexico coasts of the United States. My research aims to improve our knowledge of Forster’s Tern population structure and movement across the full annual cycle, including determining whether different subspecies exist, as was proposed more than 80 years ago. For this, I have two objectives: 1) assess population trends in relation to environmental covariates with the use of Citizen Science and remotely sensed environmental data, and 2) analyze population structure with the use of a combination of morphological and genetic data collected across their breeding populations. These analyses together will allow for a more complete understanding of Forster’s Terns, providing insight into their population status and structure across the annual cycle and helping determine whether separate populations should be treated as separate “Designatable Units” for the most optimal conservation and management strategies for the species.

Assessing toxic metal contamination and trophic-level transfer in the Deka and Lukosi rivers using fish and predatory birds as bioindicators

Presenter(s): Mavis Gavajena1, 2
Affiliation(s): 1. MSc candidate, Faculty of Agriculture, Environment and Natural Resources Management, Midlands State University
Session: 3MT - Pollution / Ecotoxicology
Time: 2026-10-06 20:36 UTC

Abstract

Freshwater ecosystems are increasingly threatened by contamination from toxic elements such as arsenic, manganese and lead, largely driven by mining, industrial discharge and agricultural activities. These pollutants can persist in aquatic environments, accumulating in sediments and entering food webs, where they pose risks to aquatic organisms and higher trophic levels. In river systems such as the Deka and Lukosi, which are influenced by anthropogenic activities, there is growing concern about the extent of contamination and its ecological implications.

Aquatic organisms, particularly fish, play a critical role in transferring contaminants through trophic interactions, while predatory aquatic birds may serve as effective bioindicators of environmental pollution due to their position at the top of the foodweb. Despite this, limited information exists on the distribution and bioaccumulation of toxic elements within these river systems and their potential magnification across trophic levels.

This study aims to investigate the concentrations of arsenic, manganese and lead in water, sediment and fish within the Deka and Lukosi rivers, and to assess their subsequent bioaccumulation in predatory aquatic birds. We hypothesize that contaminant levels will vary spatially and that bioaccumulation will increase with trophic position, reflecting biomagnification processes.

By linking environmental contamination to biological uptake across multiple trophic levels, this research seeks to provide insight into ecosystem health and the potential risks posed to aquatic biodiversity and avian species, while informing future monitoring and conservation strategies.

Balancing act: ATP supply and demand in torpid ruby-throated hummingbirds

Presenter(s): Amalie Hutchinson1, James Staples2, Chris G Guglielmo3
Affiliation(s): 1. Western University, 2. Western University, 3. Western University
Session: Session 2: Foraging Ecology and Diet
Time: 2026-10-06 15:50 UTC

Abstract

ATP supply by mitochondria and ATP demand by cellular processes is carefully regulated during active periods and during states of metabolic suppression, such as torpor. Torpor is a state of inactivity characterized by decreased metabolic rate (MR) and body temperature that can be spontaneously reversed using endogenously produced heat. To avoid the high cost of thermoregulation at night, hummingbirds use torpor. We searched for a site of metabolic suppression in the flight muscle of torpid ruby-throated hummingbirds (Archilochus colubris). We were the first to investigate mitochondrial dynamics in hummingbirds. OXPHOS was suppressed by ~20% through CI when pyruvate was the metabolic fuel, but no suppression was observed when mitochondria were fueled with a lipid-based substrate. As fat is the main source of energy during torpor, we concluded that, unlike mammalian models of torpor, whole-animal MR suppression does not involve downregulating mitochondria. Next, we investigated ATP demand pathways to determine if inhibiting SERCA or Na+/K+ ATPase drives torpor. SERCA activity did not change, however, the uncoupler sarcolipin was more highly phosphorylated. Phosphorylation inhibits sarcolipin, resulting in decreased ATP demand and heat generation. Na+/K+ ATPase activity decreased by 43% in torpor, a mechanism that could account for a significant proportion of whole-animal MR suppression. Taken together, we suggest that torpid hummingbirds suppress pathways of ATP demand in concert with passive metabolic suppression initiated by adjusting thermoregulation and allowing core body temperature to drop. These findings highlight interesting contrasts between birds and mammals and provide insight into their evolutionary paths to heterothermy.

Beyond habitat amount: Narrow seismic lines fragment habitat for a declining old-forest songbird

Presenter(s): Taylor Hart1, Juan Andrés Martinez Lanfranco2, Alessandro Franceschini3, Erin Bayne4
Affiliation(s): 1. University of Alberta, 2. University of Alberta, 3. University of Alberta, 4. University of Alberta
Session: Session 2: Habitat Use and Breeding Biology
Time: 2026-10-06 19:10 UTC

Abstract

Habitat loss and fragmentation are primary drivers of forest degradation and biodiversity decline, but disentangling their effects is challenging because they co-occur, may interact, and are perceived differently by different species, requiring species-specific definitions and explicit tests for additive and interactive effects. To quantify the effects of these processes on Black-throated Green Warblers (BTNW), a species of conservation concern in Alberta, we developed species-specific definitions of BTNW habitat (80–140 year old mixedwood forest) and fragmentation (edge density). We compared spatial occupancy models with habitat amount only, edge density only, and their additive and interactive effects using three increasingly comprehensive definitions of fragmentation (large polygonal disturbances only; adding wide linear features >8 m; and all anthropogenic disturbances including narrow 2–8 m seismic lines), at three spatial scales (7, 78, and 314 ha). The most comprehensive definition, including seismic lines, provided the best model fit at the 7 and 78 ha scales, indicating BTNW perceive even narrow disturbances as habitat boundaries. Edge density negatively affected occupancy at the two finer scales, but habitat amount became the dominant predictor at the broader 314 ha scale. Our species-specific approach revealed responses that generic classifications would likely obscure, demonstrating that conservation strategies for this species must consider spatial configuration alongside habitat amount. Given the strong negative effects of edge density and major population declines reported for BTNW in Alberta, we urge managers to minimize cumulative edge creation through harvest planning and active seismic line regeneration.

Beyond occupancy: evaluating whether forestry best management practices support successful reproduction of Endangered Williamson’s Sapsucker

Presenter(s): Tory Hartley-Cox1, Elizabeth Gow2, Emily Choy3
Affiliation(s): 1. PhD Student, McMaster University, 2. Environment and Climate Change Canada, 3. McMaster University
Session: 3MT - Species at Risk
Time: 2026-10-06 20:40 UTC

Abstract

Forest management practices designed to reduce the impacts of logging on wildlife may not always provide the habitat conditions necessary for successful reproduction. In some cases, retained habitat may continue to attract breeding individuals while failing to support reproductive success, potentially creating ecological traps. For the federally Endangered Williamson’s Sapsucker (Sphyrapicus thyroideus), forestry best management practices (BMPs) have been developed for use in British Columbia. However, these voluntary practices are applied only within the species’ known area of occupancy, and their effectiveness in supporting successful reproduction has never been evaluated. To determine whether current forestry BMPs provide habitat that supports successful reproduction, we are linking nest productivity to habitat characteristics, focusing on nesting and foraging conditions in both managed and unmanaged forests. During the 2025 and 2026 breeding seasons, occupancy surveys were conducted throughout the Princeton and Boundary regions of southern British Columbia to locate breeding territories and active nest cavities. Nest productivity was monitored using repeated visual observations, custom-built cavity cameras, and acoustic recording units to document clutch size, hatching success, fledging success, and nest failure. Habitat assessments quantified nest-tree characteristics, stand structure, and surrounding forest composition across protected forests, retention-harvested stands, and unlogged forests. This project will provide the first direct evaluation of whether current forestry BMPs support the successful reproduction of Williamson’s Sapsucker in Canada. By identifying the nesting and foraging habitat characteristics associated with nest success and failure, the results will help guide future refinements to forestry BMPs and inform recovery planning and long-term conservation of this Endangered species.

Can supplemental feeders be used to study caching behavior? A field study in black-capped chickadees (Poecile atricapillus)

Presenter(s): Ipshita Gayen1, Kimberley Mathot2
Affiliation(s): 1. PhD Student, University of Alberta, 2. University of Alberta; Canada Research Chair, Integrative Ecology
Session: Session 2: Foraging Ecology and Diet
Time: 2026-10-06 15:40 UTC

Abstract

Small parids like black-capped chickadees (Poecile atricapillus) rely on caching (i.e. storing food for later use) as a strategy to mitigate starvation risk during winter. Although caching can occur throughout the autumn and winter, it is predicted that most intense period should occur in late summer/early fall, when natural food availability is high, daylength is long, and ambient temperatures are favourable to both meet immediate energetic needs and invest in building up caches. Quantifying caching in free-living birds is time consuming, and thus, relatively few studies have quantified seasonal peaks in caching. Here, we assess if feeders can be used to capture seasonal peaks in caching investment using automated detections of black-capped chickadees fitted with passive integrated transponders to quantify caching in late summer -early fall. From previous studies and theoretical calculations, the number of sunflower seeds required to meet daily energetic requirements at this time of year is estimated to be ~45 seeds, and thus, daily feeder visits in excess of 45 can be inferred to indicate caching. We tested theoretical predictions about seasonal caching peaks, the effect of temperature and differences between sexes. We found that both day in season and temperature play important roles in shaping seasonal caching patterns as predicted, but contrary to theoretical predictions, females invested less in caching than males. Further, the observed seasonal peak in caching using feeders was much shorter compared caching peaks in populations without supplemental food. Our findings demonstrate that feeder-based studies can capture biologically meaningful variation in caching behaviour, with some important caveats, and highlight the combined influence of seasonal and short-term energetic factors on food-caching strategies.

Classification of egg laying, incubation and brood rearing from remotely collected telemetry data

Presenter(s): Ilsa Griebel1, Érika G da Rocha2, Abigail Butler3, Laura R Dykstra4, Cassidy L Waldrep5, John M Coluccy; Patrick K Devers6, Min T Huang7, Tracy A G Rittenhouse8, Joshua C Stiller9, Jacob N Straub10, Mitch D Weegman11
Affiliation(s): 1. University of Saskatchewan, 2. University of Saskatchewan, 3. SUNY Brockport, 4. University of Connecticut, 5. University of Saskatchewan, 6. Ducks Unlimited, Inc, 7. USFS, 8. Connecticut Department of Energy and Environmental Protection, 9. University of Connecticut, 10. New York State Department of Environmental Conservation, 11. SUNY Brockport, 12. University of Saskatchewan
Session: Session 1: Methods Matter
Time: 2026-10-06 16:50 UTC

Abstract

Knowledge of reproductive attempts and outcomes is imperative for understanding drivers of population growth and learning how individual decisions and environmental conditions explain variation in productivity, but breeding locations in remote areas of the planet can hinder our ability to monitor reproductive attempts using traditional methods. The goal of this study was to develop a data-driven, machine-learning method that identified egg laying, incubation and brood rearing behaviour for two species of dabbling duck, American black ducks (Anas rubripes) and mallards (A. platyrhynchos), to measure reproductive success remotely using Global Positioning System (GPS) and tri-axial acceleration (ACC) data collected by tracking devices. We successfully developed an egg laying/early incubation model (classified as nesting or not), a full-term incubation model (non-incubating, failed incubation attempt or successful incubation attempt) and early (1-15 days post-hatch) and late (16-30 days post-hatch) brood rearing models (combined output as failed or fledged brood), which all had overall accuracies >94%. We applied our method to two large-scale datasets collected in eastern North America on black ducks and mallards and summarized data by species, age and year. We found mallards had greater breeding success than black ducks, due to higher nest propensity and brood fledging success. For each species, after second year females had higher nest propensity, nest success, and brood fledging success than second year females. Our method can be used to understand multiple fine-scale metrics of reproductive success using retrospective analysis of tracking data, should be applicable to most dabbling duck species, and could be adjusted for other taxa equipped with tracking devices that display restricted movements during breeding. Results from this approach allow hypothesis tests of environmental drivers of productivity and evaluations of their contributions to population change.

Colour Preferences in the Selection of Anthropogenic Nesting Materials in European Starlings (Sturnus vulgaris)

Presenter(s): KC Collings1, Colleen Barber2, Gabrielle Armstrong3, Katelyn Eisner4, Olivia Smith5, Freya Binney6
Affiliation(s): 1. Saint Mary’s University, 2. Saint Mary’s University, 3. Saint Mary’s University, 4. Saint Mary’s University, 5. Saint Mary’s University, 6. Saint Mary’s University
Session: Session 1: Urban Ecology
Time: 2026-10-06 15:30 UTC

Abstract

Urban birds often incorporate anthropogenic materials into their nests. However, the factors that determine material selection remain unclear. Visual cues, such as colour, may influence birds’ choice of nesting material. European Starlings (Sturnus vulgaris), are a cavity-nesting songbird species observed to incorporate anthropogenic materials into their nests. Males build most of the nest as means to attract a mate and females help finish the construction. Although materials can be selected and added to nests, they can also later be thrown out rather than being further incorporated. This study investigates whether wild starlings exhibit a preference for certain colours of anthropogenic nesting materials. We provided birds with equal opportunity to select ribbons of five different colours (blue, gold, black, silver and white) and quantified both the colours selected from the environment and those incorporated into nests. Preliminary results indicate that certain colours are selected more often than others. Understanding the mechanisms underlying anthropogenic material selection in birds’ nests will improve our understanding of behavioural adaptations in urban environments and better inform conservation strategies.

Diet of Bobolinks (Dolichonyx oryzivorus) during fall staging

Presenter(s): Charlotte Toner1, Wendy Monk2, Joe Nocera3
Affiliation(s): 1. University of New Brunswick, 2. University of New Brunswick, 3. University of New Brunswick
Session: Session 2: Foraging Ecology and Diet
Time: 2026-10-06 16:30 UTC

Abstract

Agricultural ecosystems present complex trade-offs between human and conservation priorities, especially for migratory or at-risk species whose life cycles depend on managed habitats. Many avian species can provide valuable ecosystem services in agroecosystems, such as pest and weed control, yet these roles are often poorly quantified, especially during energetically demanding periods such as prior to migration. The Bobolink (Dolichonyx oryzivorus) is a long-distance migratory grassland passerine and species-at-risk with a distinct knowledge gap surrounding its dietary function within ecosystems, as well as its dietary patterns during pre-migratory staging. My study examines Bobolink diet during the post-breeding, pre-migratory staging period of July and August. I collected fecal samples across eight agricultural sites in the Annapolis Valley of Nova Scotia, Canada. Using DNA metabarcoding of fecal samples, I identified the dietary composition of plants and insects over time, between age groups, and in relation to plant origin and insect pest status. My results indicate that the Bobolink consumes a diverse dietary community of both plants and insects, including weeds and pests. This composition shifted significantly over time as migration approached, and insect dietary composition differed significantly between adults and juveniles. This study provides the first DNA metabarcoding analysis of the Bobolink, as well as the first fine-scale identification of the fall staging diet of the Bobolink. Together, these findings indicate that the Bobolink may contribute ecosystem services within agroecosystems while simultaneously shifting its diet to meet the energetic demands of migratory staging. By contributing to the knowledge of the Bobolink during a potentially limiting period of the life cycle, I hope to improve conservation management techniques by identifying important prey sources and emphasizing the value of this species-at-risk in agricultural ecosystems.

Disrupted Nights, Disrupted Minds: Effects of ALAN on What, Where, and When Memory in Black-capped Chickadees

Presenter(s): Aastha Saini1, Scott A MacDougall-Shackleton2
Affiliation(s): 1. Department of Biology, University of Western Ontario; Advanced Facility for Avian Research, University of Western Ontario, 2. Department of Psychology, University of Western Ontario; Advanced Facility for Avian Research, University of Western Ontario
Session: 3MT - Behaviour / Behavioural ecology
Time: 2026-10-06 20:00 UTC

Abstract

Daily rhythms of light and dark regulate most biological processes and synchronize our internal physiology with the external environment. Artificial light at night (ALAN) has become a rapidly growing environmental pollutant, disrupting these rhythms. This disruption can adversely impact humans and wildlife, especially by disturbing the sleep-wake cycle, leading to cognitive implications that impact memory consolidation. Our research investigates the impact of ALAN on memory consolidation in black-capped chickadees (Poecile atricapillus), a passerine songbird that relies heavily on spatial memory for winter survival. Previous studies have revealed that black-capped chickadees show what, where, and when memory. For my study, I manipulated the light-dark cycles and monitored the changes in their cognition by performing WWW memory tasks. Initially, the birds searched for sunflower seeds and mealworms in trees in an indoor aviary. Then these birds were allowed to revisit those sites after either short (3 hrs) or long (125 hrs) retention intervals, and their preference for food type was recorded. We hypothesized that birds would prefer mealworms during SRI and seeds during LRI, as the mealworms in LRI were degraded, and also higher light intensity will impair their WWW memory. Preliminary data showed that control birds preferred mealworms in their first four choices for both SRI and LRI, but this was not true for the other two treatments. Further data collection is needed to confirm these trends. By demonstrating that low-intensity light pollution can degrade sleep and memory in a diurnal species, this study highlights the need to reevaluate how artificial lighting affects the cognition and behaviour of wildlife.

Distinguishing migratory stopover patterns across Blackpoll Warbler populations

Presenter(s): Jax Nasimok1, Maggie MacPherson2
Affiliation(s): 1. MSc Student, Trent University, 2. Trent University
Session: Session 1: Migration
Time: 2026-10-06 19:10 UTC

Abstract

Blackpoll Warblers (Setophaga striata; blackpolls) are one of the fastest declining passerines in North America. We lack clarity on which conservation issues are responsible for their documented decline of 92% in the past 50 years. The crosswise long-distance migration pattern of blackpolls is unique among neotropical migrants. From migration theory, we should expect that populations with longer migrations will face higher mortality rates. However, recent research has discovered the opposite. Eastern breeding blackpoll populations migrate the shortest distance and are declining significantly faster than western populations. Here, I extend what we know about blackpoll migration by using the Motus network to determine what habitat blackpolls are using during their southward migration. As part of long-term research on a collaborative research team, Motus tags are being deployed on blackpolls in Alaska, Manitoba, Québec, and Newfoundland. I will present an assessment of migration habitat quality that uses remote sensing data to quantify habitat characteristics surrounding Motus towers that blackpolls from each population pass by during their post-breeding migrations south. Future work will assess how apparent annual survival is correlated with different paces of migration, migration habitat quality, and wintering location from across the blackpoll range. Insights from this research will support regionally targeted conservation actions to improve outcomes for this rapidly declining songbird.

Do black-capped chickadees behave as predicted by optimal sampling models when tracking food availability?

Presenter(s): Megan LaRocque1, Jan Wijmenga2, Kimberley Mathot3
Affiliation(s): 1. University of Alberta, 2. University of Alberta, 3. University of Alberta
Session: Session 2: Foraging Ecology and Diet
Time: 2026-10-06 16:20 UTC

Abstract

Animals face uncertainty in every aspect of their lives. Where is the best territory? Who is the best available mate? When and where will food be available? To mitigate this uncertainty, one strategy organisms can adopt is to ‘sample’; updating their information about the state of territories, mates, and/or resources within their environment. Optimal sampling models ask: what sampling strategy should individuals adopt to maximize their fitness? In a foraging context, we can imagine an individual that encounters a patch that has recently become unprofitable. This individual must then decide how it should behave amidst this uncertainty in food availability. Should it revisit (i.e., sample) this patch later to see if the patch becomes profitable, or should it visit an alternative food patch? If an individual samples the previously encountered unprofitable patch, and the patch remains unprofitable, the individual suffers from sampling error costs. If an individual instead visits an alternative patch, but the initially unprofitable patch is now profitable, the individual misses out on exploiting this now profitable patch and experiences missed opportunity costs. Theory predicts that both sampling error and missed opportunity costs will influence individual sampling frequency. We tested these predictions using feeder visit data from a free-living winter population of black-capped chickadees (Poecile atricapillus). We assessed how chickadee sampling frequency at empty feeders changed as a function of several variables predicted a priori to affect sampling error and/or missed opportunity costs. We found that although some factors influenced sampling frequency as predicted by optimal sampling theory, others did not. I discuss these results and propose explanations for how individual differences in sampling behaviour may influence fitness in our chickadee population.

Time: 2026-10-06 16:00 UTC

Abstract

Group living provides many potential benefits but can also incur important costs via competition for access to resources. Even in the absence of resource depletion, interference competition can reduce access to resources, and interference competition is not expected to affect all group members equally. In groups with dominance hierarchies, dominant individuals have priority access to resources and are therefore expected to experience lower costs of interference competition compared to subordinate individuals. We observed foraging behaviour in a population of 126 marked black-capped chickadees (Poecile atricapillus) at the University of Alberta Botanic Garden, near Edmonton, AB. We assessed the effect of manipulating the number of feeders available with ad libitum food on chickadee feeding territories, which we assumed would affect competition intensity, on total daily feeder visits. As predicted, we found strong evidence of interference competition: reducing feeder availability by 50% reduced average per capita daily visits to feeders in our population by circa 50%. We found that reductions in total daily feeder visits under lower manipulated food availability were similar for all age-sex categories, suggesting that females and immatures did not experience higher interference competition despite their subordinate status relative to males and adults. Post-hoc analyses revealed that adult females expanded their foraging area, while immature females increased both the length of their foraging window and their foraging area, when the availability of feeders was reduced. We suggest that these results may indicate that females invest more to mitigate competition and manage unpredictable access to food due to their subordinate status.
